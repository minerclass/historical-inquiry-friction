Part 1 · Media ecology in ten minutes

AI changes the information environment, not just the toolbox.

Printing press · 1440sDemocratized access and created new literacy demands.
Broadcast · 1950sCentralized narratives and made collective memory possible.
Social platforms · 2000sAlgorithmic curation and filter bubbles.
Generative AI · 2020sAlgorithmic generation and uncertain provenance.
The shift is from machines that curate symbolic content to machines that also create it.

Dissertation framework

Why “tertiary algorithmicity”

Ong (1982/2002)

Orality, literacy, secondary orality. Media change how people think and communicate.

Stalder (2016/2018)

Algorithmicity. Algorithms select, organize, and give form to cultural material.

This study

Tertiary algorithmicity. Algorithmic systems both curate and generate symbolic content, making human authorship optional at scale.

A qualitative shift calls for new leadership frameworks, not only new classroom rules.

Part 1 · The stance

Technoskeptical leadership refuses both technophobia and techno-utopianism.

Demand proof

Ask what evidence shows a tool supports the learning social studies is for.

Build safeguards

Set boundaries for data privacy, authorship, and evidence before adoption, not after.

Keep coherence

Make policy, professional learning, assessment, and communication point the same direction.

Not a ban and not a rollout. A stance that asks better questions first.

Part 3 · The central concept

Infrastructure friction

Intentional system design, through policy, professional learning, and assessment, that maintains productive struggle at the organizational level, not just in one classroom.

Classroom friction

One teacher protects the struggle in one task, often against the pacing guide.

Infrastructure friction

The system makes room for that struggle in every classroom: time, guidance, assessment, and permission.

Part 3 · Four domains

Where leaders can build infrastructure friction

Policy and purpose

Purpose statements that say what AI is and is not for in social studies. Tool boundaries: approved, not approved, and the data privacy basics. Minimum expectations: credibility routines, evidence requirements, and student voice.

Example: policy language that requires human-authored primary sources for historical inquiry, with its rationale.

Part 3 · Four domains, one direction

Do your four domains point the same way?

Policy

Does it say what AI is for in our discipline, not only what is banned?

Professional learning

Does it build judgment about sources and evidence, not just tool skill?

Assessment

Does it see the reasoning process, or only the finished product?

Communication

Could a parent or board member explain why this friction matters?

Coherence is the friction. When one domain contradicts another, teachers default to speed.

Part 4 · Guided design sprint

Draft your Social Studies + GenAI Leadership Brief

1. Purpose statement

Two or three sentences. What is AI for, and not for, in our social studies classrooms?

2. Minimum expectations

Three to five bullets. What must every teacher require of students when AI is involved?

3. Tool boundaries

A short list. What is approved and not approved? What is the data privacy floor?

4. Implementation support

Action steps. What professional learning, assessment guidance, and communication do teachers need from us?

Part 6 · Research in progress · No findings yet

What my dissertation study is asking

RQ2 · Conditions

How do educators and school-system leaders make sense of and respond to the policy, professional-learning, assessment, access, and governance conditions surrounding generative AI?

RQ3 · Supports

What language, assessment expectations, instructional practices, professional supports, and leadership approaches do educators and school-system leaders identify as important for preserving forms of friction that support learning while reducing unnecessary barriers?

The study is collecting data through February 2027. These are the questions, not findings.
