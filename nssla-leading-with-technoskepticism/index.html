Part 1 · Media ecology

AI changes the information environment, not just the toolbox.

Printing press · 1440sDemocratized access and created new literacy demands.
Broadcast · 1950sCentralized narratives and gave mass audiences shared reference points.
Social platforms · 2000sAlgorithmic curation of what each person sees.
Generative AI · 2020sAlgorithmic generation and uncertain provenance.
The shift is from machines that curate symbolic content to machines that also create it.

Dissertation framework

Why “tertiary algorithmicity”

Walter Ong (1982/2002)
Proposed in this study, building on Stalder (2016/2018)
I

Primary orality

Knowledge lives in memory and voice.

II

Literacy and print

Writing holds the word still, so it can be revisited and compared.

III

Secondary orality

Broadcast carries a voice to distant listeners, built on writing and print.

IV

Algorithmic secondary orality

People still write the words. Algorithms decide whose words circulate.

V

Tertiary algorithmicity

Algorithms also generate the words. Human authorship becomes optional at scale.

A qualitative shift calls for new leadership frameworks, not only new classroom rules.
Stages IV and V are proposals, not established terms · Take home: play Echoes5
One theory beat, about three minutes with the clip. Ong named the first three stages. Stalder’s algorithmicity, in which algorithms select, organize, and give form to cultural material, is the bridge to the two stages this study proposes. Move on once leaders can say the difference between curating and generating. Optional: Play Five Stages runs 30 seconds with chiptune music and ends on “Which stage are your students learning in?”
Part 1 · The stance

Technoskeptical leadership refuses both technophobia and techno-utopianism.

Demand proof

Ask what evidence shows a tool supports the learning social studies is for.

Build safeguards

Set boundaries for data privacy, authorship, and evidence before adoption, not after.

Keep coherence

Make policy, professional learning, assessment, and communication point the same direction.

Not a ban and not a rollout. A stance that asks better questions first.
Draws on Postman (1998); Krutka, Metzger, & Seitz (2022); Civics of Technology6
This is the stance the rest of the session puts into practice. Innovation is welcome; it has to earn its place in a discipline built on evidence.

Part 1 · Put the questions to work

Debate it: is a ban enough?

The pouch

A restriction sets a boundary. It can change access, interruption, and the visible norms around attention.

The bypass

The same habit moves to the next available screen, often one the school issued. Removing one screen does not redesign the ecology.

Motion: a bell-to-bell phone ban will improve learning in our social studies classrooms. One side argues for it. The other answers with question 4: what unintended or unexpected changes follow, and on which screen?
From The Pouch and the Bypass and Screen Time Is the Wrong Question · Take home: play Trailhead9
Two minutes. Pick one motion, split the room in half, give each side 45 seconds with a neighbor, then ask for one sentence from each side. The point is the move, not the verdict: restriction creates a boundary; leadership, design, and partnership create change. The pouch and bypass language comes from the IETL talk The Pouch and the Bypass; the screen-practice question comes from the CoSN post Screen Time Is the Wrong Question. Part 3 · The central concept

Infrastructure friction

Intentional system design, through policy, professional learning, and assessment, that maintains productive struggle at the organizational level, not just in one classroom.

Classroom friction

One teacher protects the struggle in one task, often against the pacing guide.

Infrastructure friction

The system makes room for that struggle in every classroom: time, guidance, assessment, and permission.

Part 3 · Four domains

Where leaders can build infrastructure friction

Policy and purpose

Purpose statements that say what AI is and is not for in social studies. Tool boundaries: approved, not approved, and the data privacy basics. Minimum expectations: credibility routines, evidence requirements, and student voice.

Example: policy language that requires human-authored primary sources for historical inquiry, with its rationale.

Part 3 · Four domains, one direction

Do your four domains point the same way?

Policy

Does it say what AI is for in our discipline, not only what is banned?

Professional learning

Does it build judgment about sources and evidence, not just tool skill?

Assessment

Does it see the reasoning process, or only the finished product?

Communication

Could a parent or board member explain why this friction matters?

Coherence is the friction. When one domain contradicts another, teachers default to speed.

Part 4 · Try it

One task, four decisions

Pick one task your teachers assign, such as a DBQ, a seminar, or a current events analysis. Turn to a neighbor and make four calls.

Keep

What thinking must stay human in this task?

Scaffold

Where could AI support access without doing the thinking?

Shift

What could move to AI so time goes to harder work?

Remove

Which friction here is only a barrier?

Then ask: which of your four domains would have to change for that call to hold in every classroom?

Part 5 · Research in progress · No findings yet

What my dissertation study is asking

RQ2 · Conditions

How do educators and school-system leaders make sense of and respond to the policy, professional-learning, assessment, access, and governance conditions surrounding generative AI?

RQ3 · Supports

What language, assessment expectations, instructional practices, professional supports, and leadership approaches do educators and school-system leaders identify as important for preserving forms of friction that support learning while reducing unnecessary barriers?

Data collection continues into early 2027. These are the questions, not findings.
